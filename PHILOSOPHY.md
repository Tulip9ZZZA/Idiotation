# PHILOSOPHY.md — The Mental Model of Finding the Right Ideas

> **A good idea is a bet with real structure, shaped to the specific gate it has to
> pass, that gets stronger every time it passes.**

Everything else — elegance, surprise, compression, one-sentence frameworks — is
technique in service of that fit. This document is the constitution Idiotation judges
every idea against. It is applied through the `/good-idea` lens at every gate of the
kill-chain.

## 1. Where the definition comes from

A good idea is one that **changes what you do next** — and, better, lets you tell the
good version from the bad one *before* you build it. Four cuts, all parallel:

- **Good = load-bearing, not decorative.** A nice idea is something you nod at. A good
  idea is something other decisions rest on.
- **Good = a compression that generates rules.** A lossy but useful summary of
  experience — cheap to state, expensive to rediscover.
- **Good = checkable, not aspirational.** "Keep it short" can't be verified. "Under a
  screen" can. A hard edge beats a vibe, because a good idea makes its own failure visible.
- **Good = fitness to a purpose, not intrinsic.** Every idea declares its own domain
  of validity.

The sharpest version: **a good idea is one that survives contact with a decision.**
It makes a prediction about what will work, and that prediction can lose. Ideas that
can't lose aren't ideas — they're moods.

## 2. Fit to a mechanism — the novel shelf and the Mona Lisa

"Works" is always works-*for-a-gate*. The novelist's one-sentence hook — stake +
cliffhanger + concrete situation, processable in a single glance — is not a law of
storytelling. It is the shape of the shelf: a book is bought by a glance, so the idea
must survive a glance. **The framework is isomorphic to the purchasing decision.**
The mechanism you optimize for selects which ideas look good to you:

> **You don't find good ideas — you choose the mechanism, and the mechanism tells
> you what counts as good.**

The Mona Lisa proves the relativity harder than you'd think. The painting didn't
change between 1500 and 1900 — the gate did. Patronage didn't buy depth; the
museum/auction/canon gate did. So a good idea can be **early** (correct for a
mechanism that doesn't exist yet), **dead** (correct for one that just closed), or
**works-somewhere-specific** (the only honest meaning of "works").

Nvidia/AI are the other species: they don't match one gate — they **lower the cost of
passing every gate**. Mirror failure modes: a universal multiplier too vague to act on
(good idea, no buyer) vs a gate-matched pitch that only works for one gate (sells the
book, nobody finishes it).

## 3. The four parts

### 3.1 Structure — it can be wrong
"Just help people" isn't an idea; nobody is against it, so it carries no information.
The flood ladder — monthly → weekly → daily prediction on satellites; hours breaks
satellites, so drones enter — is good precisely because **each rung names where the
previous rung breaks.** A good idea stakes something: drones cost money, coverage is
thin, latency might not beat the rain. Structure you can test.

### 3.2 Fit — it is shaped to a gate
Nothing works in the abstract. Name the gate, then check the shape against it.

### 3.3 Surface — the structure shows
The Mona Lisa's depth was *made* by technique; the depth is real AND it reads on the
surface. Surface only = a pitch. Structure only = invisible, and invisible ideas
don't work no matter how true they are. **The real thing and the sellable thing are
the same thing.**

### 3.4 Loop — it compounds
Collect the data, retrain, predict better, collect more. **The ladder is the wedge;
the dataset is the moat.** Without the loop you have a novel — one-time sale. With
it, each pass makes the next pass cheaper.

## 4. The test

Four questions, and a good idea survives all four:

1. **What would make this wrong?** (structure)
2. **Who is the gate, and does this fit the shape of that gate?** (fit)
3. **Can someone see it in a glance and still find the depth when they lean in?** (surface)
4. **What gets better the second time?** (loop)

Fail one → still a **nice** idea. Fail two → **bad**. Fail three → **mood**.

## 5. Bad idea — one failure, six angles

- **Unfalsifiable.** Can't lose, so it can't win.
- **Gate-blind.** Real structure, wrong shape for the filter. Brilliant and unsellable.
- **Surface-only.** The glance passes; nothing is behind it.
- **No loop.** Works once, then you start over. A demo, not a business.
- **Imaginary gate.** Looks like vision, is actually noise — unless the gate is *coming*.
- **Generic where specific was possible.** The most common hackathon failure: saying
  what everyone is supposed to say, indistinguishable from saying nothing.

## 7. The three disciplines that keep it honest

1. **Facts are acquired, not asserted.** Firecrawl (or your fetch tools) are the only
   source of facts. The models interpret; they do not invent. Exact URL + date for
   every material claim. Honest UNKNOWN > guessed fact.
2. **Structured disagreement, not consensus.** The council surfaces; the lens ranks.
   Majority is not evidence. Dissent is preserved — it is raw material for the next gate.
3. **A spike beats an argument.** Every load-bearing assumption gets a 30-minute test
   against the real system. "Not tested" is never "passed."

**The fundamental thing:** a good idea is a *fit between real structure and a specific
filter, visible on the surface, that compounds.* Everything else is technique in
service of that fit.
